You know when you're somewhere loud, a song comes on that you recognize, and you can't remember what it's called? You hold your phone up for a few seconds and Shazam tells you the name of it.

I've used it for years and never really thought about how it works, so I looked into it. The main idea comes from a paper Avery Wang wrote back in 2003, and it doesn't use AI at all. It's a fairly simple idea once you see it, so let me walk you through it.

The first thing you might think of is comparing your recording directly against every song until you find the one that matches. The problem is that sound is stored as a wave, which is really just a long list of numbers, and those numbers change with almost everything. If someone's talking over the music, or you started recording halfway through the song, your recording ends up looking very different from the original. On top of that, comparing raw audio against millions of songs would be really slow.

So Shazam needed a kind of fingerprint for each song, something small that's quick to look up and still holds up when there's noise in the background.

The first step is to turn the sound into a picture. Any sound is made up of lots of pitches playing at the same time, from the low bass up to the high cymbals, and there's a bit of math called the Fourier transform that separates them out. If you do that for every small slice of time, you get what's called a spectrogram. Time runs from left to right and pitch runs from bottom to top, and the brighter a spot is, the louder that pitch was at that moment.

Next, Shazam throws away most of that picture and only keeps the peaks, which are the spots that are louder than everything around them. What's left is a scatter of dots that looks a bit like stars, and the paper actually calls it a constellation map.

The reason this helps is that background noise tends to be spread out and fairly quiet compared to the main notes in a song. It fills in the darker parts of the picture, but it usually doesn't drown out the peaks. And if the volume is lower, the peaks get dimmer but stay in the same place. So even in a noisy room, a lot of the dots in your recording will match the dots in the original song.

A single dot doesn't tell you much on its own, though, since lots of songs will have the same pitch at some point. So Shazam pairs each dot with a few of the dots that come shortly after it. For each pair it writes down the two pitches and the time between them. That combination is much more specific than a single dot, and it's what Shazam uses as a fingerprint.

Shazam has already done this for every song in its library. It stores each fingerprint along with which song it came from and where in the song it appears. When you record something, your phone runs the same process on your few seconds of audio and sends the fingerprints to Shazam's servers, which is quicker than sending the audio itself.

The servers then look up your fingerprints. Some will match the right song, but plenty will match other songs by coincidence, so at first you get matches spread across lots of different songs.

To figure out which one is right, Shazam looks at timing. For each match, it compares where the fingerprint appears in the song with where it appeared in your recording. Say you started recording 62 seconds into the song. Something from 1 second into your clip would be at 63 seconds in the song, something from 2 seconds in would be at 64, and so on. For the right song, all of the matches are off by that same 62 seconds.

The coincidental matches don't line up like that, so their time differences are all over the place. If you chart the time differences for each song, the wrong songs give you a fairly flat spread, while the right song gives you one tall spike at 62 seconds. That spike is how Shazam knows it's found your song. It's also why noise isn't a big problem, because even if a lot of your fingerprints get lost, the ones that are left still point to the same spot.

So that's the whole process. Shazam turns the sound into a picture, keeps the loudest points, pairs them up into fingerprints, and then looks for the song where the timing of those fingerprints lines up.

That's still the basic idea behind Shazam today, more than twenty years after the paper came out, and Apple bought the company in 2018. If you liked this one, I also made a video on the algorithm behind Google Maps, which you might enjoy too.
