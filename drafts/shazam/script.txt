Okay, so you're in a bar. It's loud. Someone behind you is telling a story way too loudly, and there's this song playing that you know you know. You just can't name it.

So you pull out your phone, hold it up like an idiot for five seconds, and Shazam just tells you. Song, artist, album. Done.

I've done this probably a thousand times and never once stopped to think about how insane it is. Your phone heard five seconds of muffled audio, with a guy yelling over it, and picked the right song out of tens of millions.

And it isn't AI doing that. The core idea comes from a short paper written in 2003, and honestly, it's so clever it's a little annoying. By the end of this video you'll be able to explain it to a friend at that same bar. They might not want you to. But you'll be able to.

So my first guess, and probably yours, is that it just compares the sounds. Take your recording, slide it along every song, and see which one matches.

That doesn't work. Not even close.

A recording is a wave, which is basically a long list of numbers. Turn the volume down and every number changes. Add the loud guy and every number changes again. Start recording halfway through the chorus and now you don't even know where to line it up. Your recording and the original song look like they've never met.

And even if it did work, comparing raw audio against tens of millions of songs would take, I don't know, forever. Your drink would be warm.

So Shazam needed something else. A fingerprint. Something tiny, something you can look up fast, and something a loud bar can't wreck.

The first move is to stop looking at the wave at all.

Any sound you hear is a bunch of pitches stacked on top of each other. The bass, the vocals, the hi-hats, all at once. There's a bit of math called the Fourier transform that pulls them back apart. It's like taking a smoothie and getting the strawberries back out. Which you can't do with an actual smoothie. Math can, though.

Do that for every tiny slice of a second and you get a picture called a spectrogram. Time goes left to right. Pitch goes bottom to top. The brighter a spot is, the louder that pitch is right then.

So now the song isn't a squiggle anymore. It's an image. And images are a lot easier to work with.

Then Shazam does something that feels completely wrong. It throws away almost the whole picture.

It only keeps the peaks. The loudest little spots, the places where a note really pokes out above everything around it. Everything else goes black.

What you're left with looks like a night sky. Dots scattered across the dark. The paper actually calls it a constellation map, which I love.

And this is why it works in that bar. The guy yelling is loud to you, but across the whole picture his voice is smeared out and kind of quiet. He fills in the dark parts. He almost never beats the song's loudest notes. And if you turn the volume down, the stars get dimmer, but they don't move.

So the stars in your noisy little recording are mostly the same stars as in the clean song. Not all of them. Enough of them.

One star on its own is useless, though. It's just a pitch at a moment, and about a million songs have that exact pitch somewhere.

So Shazam does what people have been doing with the night sky forever. It connects the dots.

It picks a star, then pairs it with a few stars that come just after it. Each pair turns into three numbers. The first pitch, the second pitch, and how much time sits between them.

Three little numbers. That's a fingerprint. And it's way more specific than one star, because now you need two pitches, in that order, that exact distance apart.

Shazam did this to every song in its library ahead of time. Every pair, from every song, saved with a little note saying which song it came from and when it shows up.

When you hold up your phone, it does the same thing to your five seconds. Picture, stars, pairs. Then it sends just those numbers off to Shazam. Not the audio. Just the numbers. Which is a big part of why it's so quick.

Okay. Now the server looks up your fingerprints, and this is where it gets messy.

Some of them match the right song. But loads of them also match random songs by pure coincidence, and the loud guy adds a few fake ones too. So you get hits scattered across thousands of songs. It looks like total chaos.

This next bit is my favorite part of the whole thing.

For every hit, Shazam checks one thing. When does this fingerprint show up in the song, compared to when it showed up in your recording?

Say you started recording sixty-two seconds into the song. Then a fingerprint from second one of your clip sits at second sixty-three of the song. Second two is at sixty-four. Second three is at sixty-five. Every real match is off by the same sixty-two seconds. Every single one.

The random matches don't do that. Their gaps are all over the place.

So for every song, Shazam just counts up the gaps. For the wrong songs you get a flat, boring mess. For the right song, you get one massive spike sitting right at sixty-two.

That spike is the answer. That's your song.

And even if the noise wrecked most of your fingerprints, it doesn't matter. The ones that survived all point at the same spot, and that's enough.

So, the whole thing one more time. Turn the sound into a picture. Keep only the stars. Connect them into fingerprints. Find the song where they all line up.

That's it. No giant neural network. A guy named Avery Wang published this in 2003, and the same core idea is still inside Shazam today, more than twenty years and one Apple acquisition later.

So next time you hold your phone up in some loud bar, you'll know what it's actually doing.

It's looking at the stars.
