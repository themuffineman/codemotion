# How GPS knows where you are: script

This is the explainer every model has to animate in the free-models Remotion test.
Every model gets the same voiceover and word-timed transcript; the visuals are up to them.

Audience: general tech-curious viewers, no physics background.
Length: about 3 minutes (about 440 words).
Facts used: about 31 working GPS satellites, orbiting about 20,000 km up; the signal travels at the speed of light, about 300,000 km per second, and takes about a fifteenth of a second to reach you; a clock that's off by a millionth of a second puts you about 300 m off; relativity makes the satellite clocks run about 38 microseconds a day fast.

---

## 1. Intro

When you open a map on your phone, that little blue dot knows where you are to within a few metres, and it works anywhere on Earth, even in the middle of the ocean with no signal. So in this video I want to show you how that actually works, because the idea behind it is surprisingly simple.

## 2. The satellites

There are about thirty GPS satellites going around the Earth, about twenty thousand kilometres up, and they're spread out so that wherever you're standing, at least four of them are above the horizon. Each one has a very precise atomic clock on board, and all it does, over and over, is send out a radio message that says, this is where I am, and this is exactly what time it is.

And your phone never sends anything back. It only listens.

## 3. Measuring distance with time

So your phone picks up one of those messages, and it compares the time in the message with the time when it arrived. Radio travels at the speed of light, which is about three hundred thousand kilometres every second, so if the message took a fifteenth of a second to get to you, you can work out that the satellite is about twenty thousand kilometres away.

## 4. Narrowing it down

But knowing you're twenty thousand kilometres from one satellite doesn't tell you much, because that could be anywhere on a giant sphere around it. If you add a second satellite, you have to be somewhere where the two spheres overlap, and that's a circle. Then a third satellite cuts that circle down to just two points, and one of them is usually way out in space, so you can throw it away. That leaves one point, and that's you.

## 5. The fourth satellite

There's one problem though. The clock in your phone is nowhere near as good as an atomic clock, and if it's off by just a millionth of a second, your position is off by about three hundred metres. So your phone listens to a fourth satellite as well, and it uses that extra measurement to work out how wrong its own clock is and correct for it.

## 6. Relativity

And there's one more strange detail. Because the satellites are moving so fast and they're further away from Earth's gravity, their clocks actually run a tiny bit faster than clocks down here, by about thirty eight millionths of a second every day. It sounds like nothing, but if nobody corrected for it, GPS would drift off by around ten kilometres every single day. So the satellites are set to tick slightly slow on purpose, and Einstein's relativity is part of the reason your blue dot ends up in the right place.
